# Research and sourcing craft

Every factual claim in the piece must survive a check against its source. Work in this order.

1. Find the load-bearing claims. A piece stands on a few claims: a price, a feature, a rule, a result, a date. List them. Give ordinary colour and opinion a glance only.
2. Go to the primary source. Use the product's own help, documentation, pricing or policy page, the official announcement, or the study itself.
3. Treat anything else as a lead. A blog, news story, forum, comparison site or aggregator points toward a primary source. Follow it there. Do not cite the lead as the source when a primary source exists.
4. Open the page and confirm it says the fact in the same meaning. A link that resolves is not enough. The page must say what the claim says.
5. Check currency and context. Note the date of the page and whether it has been updated or replaced. Do not use a superseded figure. Keep the claim's conditions with it: the plan, the region, the version, the sample.
6. If no primary source says it, do not state it. Say the claim could not be confirmed, or leave it out.
7. Never invent a study, a figure, a date, a quote, a URL or a step. Never present a performance result without a primary source that states it.
8. Record the date you checked each source. Show the source next to the claim, in the place the output format asks for it.

Quotes: use the exact words, with their context. Never trim a quote so that it changes its meaning.

Health, money and legal topics need extra care. State only what a primary source supports, and add a note that the piece is not professional advice where the topic calls for one.

Distinguish three kinds of statement: a sourced fact, a vendor's own claim (which is a claim, not a verified result), and the writer's own observation (which must be marked as such and must not be dressed up as research).
