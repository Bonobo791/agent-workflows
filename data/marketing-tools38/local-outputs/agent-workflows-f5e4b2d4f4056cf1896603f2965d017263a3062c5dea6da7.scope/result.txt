# How to Automate Blog Creation With AI

You can automate parts of blog creation by giving AI a defined job, supplying approved material, and routing its output to a person for review. A workable process starts with an idea and a brief, moves through evidence and drafting, and ends with a recorded human decision. It does not need to publish automatically.

For a small team, the same person may fill several roles. Name that person anyway: someone must be accountable for checking evidence and rights, making edits, and deciding whether the article is ready. AI can help organize the work and point out gaps; its output is material to inspect, not a decision to accept.

The workflow below is editorial guidance you can adapt to a shared document, spreadsheet, or task board. Start with one article and make each handoff visible before connecting tools or adding automation.

## 1. Choose an idea and set its boundaries

Collect ideas in one intake list. For each, note the reader's question, who raised it or where it came from, the intended outcome, and any existing article that might already answer it. A customer question can be a useful starting point; it is not, by itself, evidence of how often people search for the subject. If you have no demand or site data, leave those fields unknown.

Choose an idea your team can support with relevant knowledge and usable evidence. Check whether it belongs in an existing article before creating a new one. Then write a brief that makes the assignment narrow enough to review. For example, “explain our documented process” has a clearer boundary than “cover everything about the industry.”

Use this template for each assignment:

- **Working title and article purpose:**
- **Intended audience and what they already know:**
- **Reader outcome and scope:**
- **Required sections, examples and exclusions:**
- **Approved source IDs, URLs, versions and permitted use:**
- **Claims requiring verification and evidence owner:**
- **Rights requirements and unresolved permissions:**
- **Tone, format and editorial length target:**
- **Acceptance criteria, reviewer and decision owner:**
- **Brief version, date and links to dependent records:**

Make acceptance criteria observable. “The article explains the three steps in the approved process note and identifies who reviews the result” is easier to assess than “make it engaging.” Specify exclusions too: a writer should know if pricing, medical advice, or an unverified product feature is outside the assignment.

For context, Google's [people-first content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) advises creators to focus on helpful, reliable content made for people. Google also publishes guidance on [using generative AI content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content) and on [generative AI features in Google Search](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide). These are sources of Google's own guidance; they do not promise a result for this workflow.

## 2. Build an approved source set

Before drafting, have an evidence owner select the materials the article may use. Depending on the assignment, these might include an approved process note, an interview transcript, or a primary document. Record a source ID, title, URL or storage location, author or owner, publication or update date when available, date checked, and a version or preserved snapshot. Record permitted use separately. A source can support a statement without giving you permission to reproduce all of its material.

Create a claim tracker alongside the source list. Give each factual statement its own ID, identify its location in the draft, and link it to the precise passage in the particular source version. Include the reviewer, evidence status, rights status, and decision. Track a quotation's exact wording and attribution, and check that each citation actually supports the adjacent claim, including its conditions and limits. A link to a reputable home page is not a substitute for the relevant passage.

Use statuses such as **pending**, **verified**, and **blocked**. If support is missing, ambiguous, out of date for the claim, or mismatched, block the claim until a person resolves it or remove it. Treat unresolved permission in the same way. Keep notes on what the permission covers, including where the material may be used and any conditions. Do not assume a source's availability or an AI-generated citation clears rights.

A practical automation can create an empty tracker row when a draft is submitted and notify the assigned reviewer. The human still opens the source, checks the passage in context, and records the result. Preserve the source version used for the decision so a later reviewer can tell what was checked.

## 3. Draft within the approved brief and sources

Give the assistant the brief version and only the excerpts approved for the task. Ask it to distinguish sourced claims from recommendations and invented examples. You can use AI to propose an outline, organize supplied notes, draft a section, or identify questions for the evidence owner. Review the resulting text against the assignment rather than assuming it followed every instruction.

Start with this illustrative prompt and adapt it to your tools and material:

> Using only the approved brief and source excerpts supplied below, draft the article for the specified audience. Stay within the stated scope. Identify each factual claim, quotation and citation with its source ID and precise supporting passage. Mark missing or ambiguous evidence as BLOCKED; do not invent facts, quotations or citations. Separate editorial recommendations and the hypothetical example from sourced claims. Return a draft and a claim-to-source list for human review. Brief: [version and text]. Approved source excerpts: [IDs, versions and excerpts].

The claim-to-source list is a review aid. Check it against the actual draft: a model may omit a claim from its list or point to a passage that does not say what the draft says. If the source set cannot answer a necessary question, return to the evidence owner. Do not fill the gap with a plausible sentence.

Add the team's own relevant perspective where it is supported: explain a decision, show a process the team can document, or provide a clearly labeled hypothetical example. Edit the output for the intended reader, remove repetition, and check that headings and the opening answer the brief. Record the resulting draft version before review.

## 4. Review evidence, rights, and the article

A second AI pass can help make a list of potential issues. Supply the same versions used for the draft and ask for locations, not just a general assessment:

> Compare this draft against brief [version] and source set [version]. List every factual claim, quotation and citation with the exact supporting passage, or flag it BLOCKED if support is missing, ambiguous or mismatched. Flag rights questions, scope drift and dependencies affected by changes. Do not assume a citation proves a claim. Return findings with locations and proposed remedies for a human reviewer. Draft: [version]. Source excerpts and rights records: [versions].

A named reviewer then checks the draft independently against the brief, source passages, and rights records. Read quotations against the original, test links, and check whether the cited passage supports the full claim. Review headlines and descriptions as well as the body: a shortened headline can overstate a carefully qualified paragraph.

Record each finding, its location, owner, remedy, and resolution. A correction may mean revising the wording, obtaining better evidence or permission, or removing the material. Leave an item blocked if it cannot be resolved. The decision owner reviews the findings and records what was approved or blocked and why; an AI review does not replace that decision.

## 5. See how a claim moves through the tracker

The following example is invented to illustrate the process. It describes no real bakery or result.

A two-person team plans an article about a fictional bakery's bread-making process. The owner defines the scope and supplies material they are authorized to use. The editor opens a tracker with this row:

| Field | Example entry |
|---|---|
| Claim | C-01: “The bakery begins its dough preparation each morning” |
| Source | S-01, owner-provided process note v1 |
| Evidence location | Paragraph 2 |
| Reviewer | Editor |
| Evidence status | Pending verification |
| Rights | Pending confirmation |
| Decision | Blocked |

The row is a request for checks, not proof that the claim is ready. Once the owner confirms the exact statement and permission, the editor records the evidence and rights decisions before considering release. If either remains unresolved, the claim stays blocked or is removed. The editor should also check any later summary or headline that repeats it.

## 6. Adapt, package, and record the decision

After the article is reviewed, prepare channel-specific drafts from the approved article version. Give each asset its own identifier, intended destination, owner, and link to the article version. Adapt the framing to the audience while preserving the meaning and qualifications of the original. Check any new factual claim, quotation, or material introduced during adaptation against evidence and rights; approval of the article does not automatically approve a changed claim.

Check the current requirements and rules for each actual destination before considering an asset ready. If the destination or its rules are unknown, mark that channel blocked until someone checks them. For a community post, identify the community and review its current rules. Record media provenance, permission, and any required attribution alongside the asset. An editable original and a rendered version should each have their own version and review status.

Assemble the article, supporting sources and claim tracker, review findings and resolutions, channel assets, and rights records into a package for the decision owner. A package can be a linked folder and index; it does not require a particular platform. Mark which items are ready for a decision and which remain blocked. The owner can approve the reviewed package, request changes, or block it, recording their name, time, scope of decision, and rationale. A decision to prepare a package is distinct from permission to publish it.

## 7. Keep the version chain intact

Preserve identifiers or immutable snapshots and timestamps for the brief, source set and individual evidence, draft, review findings and resolutions, channel assets, package, and human decision. Link each item to the versions it depends on. A simple chain might look like this:

**Brief v1 → source set v1 and evidence snapshots → draft v1 → review v1 and resolutions → article v2 → channel assets v1 → package v1 → human decision.**

If the brief, evidence, or draft changes, identify the affected downstream items and reopen their checks. For example, a corrected source may require a claim edit, another review of the article, and changes to every channel draft that repeats the claim. Keep the prior decision and its rationale in the history rather than overwriting it; record the new decision against the new versions.

Before the decision, use this checklist:

- [ ] The audience, outcome, scope, exclusions, and acceptance criteria are explicit.
- [ ] Every factual claim, quotation, and citation has verified support in an approved, version-recorded source; unsupported material is removed or blocked.
- [ ] Rights and permissions for included material are documented; unresolved material is blocked.
- [ ] A human has reviewed the draft and recorded the decision and unresolved findings.
- [ ] Version links connect the brief, sources, draft, review, channel assets, package, and decision.
- [ ] Hypothetical material is labeled, and AI review is treated as assistance rather than verification.

Pick one article and run it through this sequence. Keep the brief, tracker, and decision record together, including anything that stayed blocked. Use what the team learns from that run to adjust the next brief and its handoffs.
